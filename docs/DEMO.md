## Part 1: The story (high-level explanation)

Tsunami warning systems in the Mediterranean currently rely on the detection of earthquakes rather than the observation of the waves themselves, as a warning centre estimates whether a tsunami is likely from the location, depth and magnitude of an earthquake and then waits for coastal tide gauges to confirm it. This is due to the Mediterranean having very few instruments at sea, with the first two deep-sea tsunami buoys only deployed off eastern Sicily and Calabria in 2025. The cost of relying on inference alone was shown on 23 October 2026, when schools in Palermo and Trapani were evacuated following a Mw 5.5 earthquake and the alert was only lifted after about an hour, once tide gauges showed no change in sea level. However, the thousands of merchant ships already at sea broadcast both their heading and their actual course over ground through AIS, and the gap between the two reflects how much the water is pushing each ship sideways.

Inazu et al. (2020) showed that this sideways drift tracked the currents of the 2011 Japanese tsunami, using real AIS data from 13 ships, with the agreement between ship drift and simulated currents rising from 0.72 to 0.78 once the apparent drift caused by ships turning had been removed. Despite this, their evidence is limited to one very large event in one sea, and their detection limit of around 0.1 to 0.3 m/s applies to a single ship at a single moment. They could not test smaller tsunamis, other seas, whole fleets acting together or the rate of false alarms, as the real data required to do so does not exist. As well as this, Hossen et al. (2021) found in a synthetic study of Cascadia that ship velocity data alone could not recover the tsunami, which suggests that a negative result is a real possibility. This project therefore seeks to address whether the ships already at sea could act as a tsunami detection network in the Mediterranean, and if so, where, for which earthquakes and how much faster than tide gauges. The aim is early offshore confirmation of large tsunamis rather than the cancellation of alerts for moderate earthquakes such as the one off Palermo, as ships that see nothing cannot prove that nothing is coming.

In order to answer this, we simulate hundreds of earthquakes along the Hellenic, Calabrian and Cyprus arcs and in the Aegean, using fault geometry from the European Fault-Source Model 2020, and combine each with realistic ship traffic, noise and three weather conditions, resulting in many thousands of trials of what each ship would report. The simulated noise is set at the levels Inazu measured after their yaw correction, and each ship's reports are then cleaned through 1-minute averaging and the subtraction of each ship's normal drift, which removes the effect of ordinary currents, steady wind and compass error. A detector then compares the cleaned reports against a library of candidate earthquakes, using a noise-weighted dot product between the reports and each candidate's predicted pattern, with ships travelling in opposite directions aligned first so that their signals add rather than cancel. It raises an alarm when the best match exceeds a threshold set from simulated days with no tsunami, and estimates the size of the earthquake from the candidate closest to the data. As we planted each tsunami ourselves, we know the true location, size and timing, which allows us to measure whether the detector found it, how quickly and how accurately. This is compared against Inazu's single-ship rule (both as published and recalibrated to the same false-alarm rate), the times at which tide gauges and the two Ionian buoys would have registered the wave, and ships fitted with precise GPS, as well as replays of the 2020 Samos and 1956 Amorgos earthquakes using today's traffic.

The parallelism lies in restructuring the problem rather than simply running the same calculations faster. Hundreds of tsunamis advance together on the GPU in a single grid update, and as the wave physics is linear at sea, each candidate earthquake only needs to be simulated once and can then be reused for every traffic arrangement, noise draw and weather condition, which turns detection into a single large matrix multiplication. Along with this, cumulative sums give the score at every minute in one pass rather than through a loop. The results are verified against a slower, simple version of the same code in order to show that the answers are identical, while the speed-up is measured against both a naive serial version and a batched version on the CPU. Early timings show a T4 GPU advancing a batch of 64 tsunamis at 0.31 seconds each, against 5.3 seconds each with NumPy on the same machine, i.e. roughly 17 times faster before the scoring is counted (provisional until the full benchmark). This speed is what makes the experiment feasible, as it allows thousands of traffic arrangements, noise conditions and weather levels to be tested, resulting in maps with error bars and a measured false-alarm rate rather than a single estimate.

The main assumptions are stated openly, the most important being that the simulated noise is taken from Inazu's figures after their yaw correction, which assumes that a real system would apply that correction. As well as this, the noise multipliers used for rough weather are placeholders, the earthquake sources are simplified shapes rather than full fault models, and the reactions of captains following an earthquake, GPS jamming in the eastern Mediterranean and internal waves in narrow straits are not modelled. Early results already show why the network approach matters, as Inazu's single-ship rule applied to a fleet of around 2,000 ships raises a false alarm on more than 99% of simulated quiet days, whereas the network detector holds a 1% rate. Given that around 78 to 80% of the Mediterranean is deeper than 200 m, where tsunami currents are likely to remain below the noise, the most likely result is that ships can only confirm large tsunamis along a limited number of shallow, busy corridors. While this is a somewhat negative headline, it is still a useful one, as it identifies where existing traffic adds value and where dedicated instruments, or ships fitted with precise GPS, would be required instead.

## Part 3: Build specification

**Owner:** Stream C (pitch materials and T22), as new ticket T27. Stream A supplies the showcase data and reviews.

**Data needed that does not exist yet:**
- **Showcase full-field snapshots.** The solver normally saves receivers only. For the animation, run the Hellenic Mw 8 showcase event and the Samos replay once more, saving the full eta field every 1 minute for 2 hours (float16, well under 1 GB). This is one extra solver call each; it can run on the Mac CPU without the GPU.
- **Showcase traffic draw.** Fix one traffic draw and one noise draw (seeded) and save, for that event, the per-ship cleaned drift, signal-to-noise, detection score over time, threshold, top three candidates and gauge and buoy trigger times.
- **Quiet-day example.** One quiet day with the same traffic: the times and positions of single-ship false alarms, and the network score staying below threshold.
- **Benchmark timings.** Read directly from `outputs/benchmark.json`; scene 12 animates at the measured rates, never invented ones.
- **Text cards.** Scenes 2 and 3 use facts from cited sources only. Keep the text and its citations in `scripts/demo/cards.yaml` so every claim on screen can be checked; anything without a source is left out.

**How to render:**
- One script per scene (`scripts/demo/scene_01.py` and so on), each reading from `outputs/` and writing `outputs/demo/scene_XX.mp4`, plus `scripts/demo/build_all.py` that renders every scene and joins them into `outputs/demo/demo_full.mp4` and `outputs/demo/demo_3min.mp4`.
- matplotlib animation (`FuncAnimation`) with ffmpeg, H.264 at `-preset veryfast`, 30 fps, 1920 x 1080.
- One shared style from `viz.py`: dark background, one colour per meaning (wave height, ship signal, alarm, instruments), large fonts readable from the back of a room, titles and units on every panel, a small caption line on each scene, and a small section label in the corner (Intro, Problem, Previous work, Solution, Data, Method, Simulation, Results, Conclusion).
- Every scene falls back to a labelled placeholder ("result pending") if its input file is missing, so the whole video can be rendered now with dev data and re-rendered once real results land.
- Also export one still per scene (`outputs/demo/scene_XX.png`) for slides, in case video playback fails.

**Order of work:**
1. Now: scenes 1 to 7 (title, problem, previous work, solution, data and method need only the grid, traffic, cited facts, diagrams and the cleaning figure) and the build pipeline with placeholders.
2. After the showcase snapshots: scenes 8, 9 and 11.
3. After T17, T18 and T19 (Sunday morning): scenes 10, 12 and 13 (scene 10 also needs the T26 Samos gauge table).
4. After T26: scene 14, then scene 15. Final render of both cuts by G5.

**Done when:** both videos render end to end from a single command, every number on screen comes from a file in `outputs/` or a cited entry in `cards.yaml`, and every scene has a matching still.
